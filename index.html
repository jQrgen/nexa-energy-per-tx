Same joules, different denominator

Nexa is a proof-of-work blockchain in the Bitcoin lineage: the same UTXO accounting and the same kind of mining, but engineered for throughput on the base layer, with native tokens and script-template contracts instead of a second layer. Its bottlenecks are the ones every UTXO chain hits on general-purpose CPUs, signature checks and coin lookups. Nexa benchmarks 42 000 transactions per second today; Blitz moves those two steps onto FPGAs, with a projected 100 000 TPS.

This figure accompanies the article 1 Bitcoin transaction: 580 kWh. 1 Nexa transaction with Blitz: 0.044 kWh. Its argument is arithmetic. A proof-of-work network burns whatever the block reward pays for, however many transactions it carries, so energy per transaction is network electricity divided by throughput. Ten years of debate have been about the numerator. The figure fixes it at the Cambridge estimate, 138 TWh a year, and moves the denominator, the only term a protocol can change. Read the axes before the lines: both are logarithmic, each gridline a factor of ten, which squeezes a 13 000× gap into four gridlines. Figure 2 repeats the data on linear axes, where the gap looks like what it is.

Further reading

Same power plants, same joules a year: what throughput changes is how many transactions those joules buy, and therefore the electricity and emissions attributable to each one. The sources below cover the two halves of that claim.

Why Nexa’s energy per transaction is lower than Bitcoin’s

  • Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance, CBECI methodology. Bitcoin’s power demand is estimated from mining-hardware efficiency, mining profitability and network hashrate. The numerator is set by mining economics, not by how many transactions a block carries.
  • Nexa, Nexa Mining. Nexapow “contributes computing power to remove two key bottlenecks in scalability: signature signing and UTXO lookups”. Those two steps are what cap throughput on every UTXO chain, and raising throughput is the only way the denominator grows.
  • 1 Bitcoin transaction: 580 kWh. 1 Nexa transaction with Blitz: 0.044 kWh, the article this figure accompanies: 42 000 TPS benchmarked today, 100 000 TPS projected with Blitz on FPGAs.

Why less energy per transaction means less environmental impact: NTNU research

  • Borgelt, J., Gilad, D., May, R. and Verones, F. (NTNU Industrial Ecology Programme and NINA), Renewable energy growth amplifies land pressure on Norwegian biodiversity, Cleaner Energy Systems 13 (2026). Building the wind, solar and grid capacity Norway needs by 2050 “could increase the overall impact on habitat loss by as much as 28%”, and “the most effective way to substantially reduce biodiversity impacts is to lower electricity demand through energy efficiency measures” (NTNU, May 2026). Clean power still costs nature; the greenest kilowatt-hour is the one you never need.
  • Hertwich, E. G., Gibon, T. et al. (NTNU), Integrated life-cycle assessment of electricity-supply scenarios confirms global environmental benefit of low-carbon technologies, PNAS 112 (20), 6277–6282 (2015). The first global life-cycle assessment of low-carbon electricity: hydro, wind and solar beat fossil generation by a wide margin but still carry land, material and emission footprints. No kilowatt-hour is free, so fewer kilowatt-hours per transaction is a smaller footprint whatever the grid mix.
  • HighEFF, the centre for industrial energy efficiency led by SINTEF Energy with NTNU, Energy efficiency first!, NTNU Energy Transition Week 2024. Norway committed at COP28 to “put the principle of energy efficiency as the first fuel at the core of policymaking, planning and investment decisions”, and “energy efficiency is a conflict-free measure that frees energy to be used for other purposes”. A payment rail that does 13 000× more per kilowatt-hour is that measure applied to money.
  • Alzoubi, Y. I. and Mishra, A. (NTNU), Green blockchain – a move towards sustainability, Journal of Cleaner Production 430 (2023). Blockchain energy use “has negative impacts on both the economy and the environment”; “the main cause of concern is the generation of atmospheric carbon emissions resulting from excessive energy usage”. For a given electricity mix, emissions per transaction scale with kilowatt-hours per transaction.
